# Confucian vs. Daoist Views of Human Nature (`道德之性與自然之性`)

An analytical essay in Chinese comparing and evaluating the Confucian (*Mengzi*, *Kongzi*) and Daoist (*Laozi*, *Zhuangzi*, *Wang Bi*) conceptions of human nature — **moral nature (`道德之性`)** versus **spontaneous naturalness (`自然之性`)**.

## Structure & Key Arguments
1. **Introduction**: Frames both schools as responses to the late-Zhou breakdown of ritual order (*Zhou wen pibi*), rooted in competing views of human nature.
2. **Comparative Analysis**:
   - **Shared Ground**: Both hold that human nature is innate (*born with it*), while moral/spiritual loss arises from acquired corruption or external artifice.
   - **Content of Human Nature**: Confucians locate innate moral sprouts (*ren, yi, li, zhi*) within the heart-mind, whereas Daoists view human nature as uncarved simplicity (*wuming zhi pu*) prior to conventional morality.
   - **Humans vs. All Things**: Confucians distinguish humans from animals by moral capacity; Daoists situate humans as equal participants within the myriad things.
   - **Realization**: Confucians cultivate and extend innate moral feelings (*kuochong*); Daoists practice *wuwei* and diminish artificial desires.
3. **Evaluation**: Argues that the Confucian view accommodates natural spontaneity (as seen in the uncalculated "sudden alarm" at a child near a well) while grounding moral responsibility and interpersonal ethics.
